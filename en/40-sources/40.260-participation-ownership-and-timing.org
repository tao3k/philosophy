These four studies examine ownership of text, ways of co-creating a poem, when help enters ideation, and opinionated writing assistance. They make “could the person's judgment change the work?” a more useful question than “does the generated text sound like them?” Tasks, samples, and measures differ; they do not add up to one product-effect estimate.

| [[https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.03283v2][Draxler et al., AI Ghostwriter Effect]], two text studies, n=30 and n=96 | Publicly claiming authorship can diverge from feeling ownership; personalization did not remove the gap, while greater influence over text was associated with greater ownership | Psychological ownership settles copyright; a human must perform most edits in all writing |
| [[https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-69423-2][McGuire et al., poetry studies]], two experiments with a particular poetry system | Editing a completed AI poem received lower creativity ratings from professional poets than writing unaided; in an iterative co-creation condition the earlier gap was not statistically significant, and creative self-efficacy also differed | Strict equivalence has been proved, or co-creation always beats delegation |
| [[https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.06197v1][Qin et al., writing ideation]], 60 people | LLM use from the start, compared with ideation before LLM use, was associated with fewer original ideas and lower creative self-efficacy and self-credit in the task | Everyone must think unaided first, or later AI use improves life over time |
| [[https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00560v1][Jakesch et al., opinion writing]], 1,506 people | An assistant favoring one side of a question about social media affected the written views and a subsequent attitude survey | Personality changed permanently, or every persuasive influence is manipulation |
* Context
The first two studies concern people's roles and experiences in particular text and poetry tasks. Claimed authorship, psychological ownership, and legal authorship are different. The poetry experiments used a purpose-built system, not every contemporary language model. Qin and colleagues compared timings in one ideation task. Jakesch and colleagues studied a writing assistant's directional bias, not a participant's “true desire.”
* Interpretation Boundaries
A nonsignificant difference does not prove strict equivalence. Expert ratings of poems and creative self-efficacy are distinct outcomes; ownership and self-credit are not long-term capability. None of these studies establishes “more edits means more agency.” Two decisive choices may set a work's direction. Someone may need extensive accessible assistance, or simply want quiet delegation. The experiments cannot judge those ways of living illegitimate.

None studied the imagined photographer in [[file:../30-reflections/30.220-making-what-matters-possible.org][the early-street scene]] or followed a work into family, community, or private life. The studies sharpen questions for observation rather than replacing it.
* Influence
[[file:../30-reflections/30.260-when-the-work-leaves-the-screen.org][When a Work Leaves the Screen]] distinguishes use of personal material, endorsement of a result, changed capability or opportunity, and real response. [[file:../30-reflections/30.230-when-help-changes-what-we-want.org][When Help Changes What We Want]] uses the opinion-writing experiment to ask how help can carry a direction. These are evidence routes for Reflections, not a new Principle or a Tao3k scenario receipt.
* Interpretation Chain
Personalization did not remove an ownership gap + editing a finished work differed from iterative co-creation
→ Familiar-sounding text and an editable interface do not, by themselves, show that a person's meaning influenced the result.

Timing affected one ideation task + an opinionated assistant affected immediate views
→ The moment help arrives and the direction it suggests must be discoverable and contestable. This is not a conclusion that AI must never influence people.
