The first four studies examine ownership of text, ways of co-creating a poem, when help enters ideation, and opinionated writing assistance; the fifth follows interviews with Chinese digital painters across years. They make “could the person's judgment change the work, and could the terms of help be renegotiated later?” more useful than “does the generated text sound like them?” Tasks, samples, and measures differ; they do not add up to one product-effect estimate.

* Context
The first two studies concern people's roles and experiences in particular text and poetry tasks. Claimed authorship, psychological ownership, and legal authorship are different. The poetry experiments used a purpose-built system, not every contemporary language model. Qin and colleagues compared timings in one ideation task. Jakesch and colleagues studied a writing assistant's directional bias, not a participant's “true desire.”

Meng and colleagues' preprint draws on annual interviews with Chinese digital painters, retaining accounts recorded in different years rather than randomly assigning a tool in a single experiment. It asks how professional and hobbyist painters reallocated tasks and authorship as image tools, peers, and client expectations changed. It gives “the terms of collaboration can change with a life” real testimony, but it does not follow the photographer imagined in this repository.
* Interpretation Boundaries
A nonsignificant difference does not prove strict equivalence. Expert ratings of poems and creative self-efficacy are distinct outcomes; ownership and self-credit are not long-term capability. None of these studies establishes “more edits means more agency.” Two decisive choices may set a work's direction. Someone may need extensive accessible assistance, or simply want quiet delegation. The experiments cannot judge those ways of living illegitimate.

The interview study is a recent preprint with a small, self-selecting cohort; four participants lacked all five waves. Its authors note recall and repeated-interview effects, and cannot isolate tool affordances from identity and industry pressure. These accounts are not a population trend for every creator, nor proof that AI caused a particular person's later change.

None of the five studies examined the imagined photographer in [[file:../30-reflections/30.220-making-what-matters-possible.org][the early-street scene]] or traced his album into family, community, or private life. They sharpen questions for observation rather than provide a Tao3k practice receipt.
* Influence
[[file:../30-reflections/30.260-when-the-work-leaves-the-screen.org][When a Work Leaves the Screen]] distinguishes use of personal material, endorsement of a result, changed capability or opportunity, and real response, then asks whether the next practice can reopen the terms of help. [[file:../30-reflections/30.230-when-help-changes-what-we-want.org][When Help Changes What We Want]] uses the opinion-writing experiment to ask how help can carry a direction. These are evidence routes for Reflections, not a new Principle or a Tao3k scenario receipt.
* Interpretation Chain
Personalization did not remove an ownership gap + editing a finished work differed from iterative co-creation
→ Familiar-sounding text and an editable interface do not, by themselves, show that a person's meaning influenced the result.

Timing affected one ideation task + an opinionated assistant affected immediate views
→ The moment help arrives and the direction it suggests must be discoverable and contestable. This is not a conclusion that AI must never influence people.

Longitudinal interviews described different paths of delegation and refusal, shaped partly by working relations
→ Terms agreed for one project cannot freeze later creation; nor does this prove everyone should renegotiate like the painters.
