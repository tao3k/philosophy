This note reads five entries in [[https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/日知錄_(四庫全書本)/卷19][volume 19 of the Siku Quanshu recension of Gu Yanwu's Record of Daily Knowledge]]: “Writing Must Benefit the World,” “Writing Is Not Valued for Its Quantity,” “The Difficulty of Writing a Book,” “Speaking Frankly,” and “Writing Beyond One's Own Time.” These English titles are working translations. The text checked was the Wikisource transcription, not a collation of page images and editions. The questions about AI below are our extensions, not ancient predictions.

[[file:../README.org][English navigation]] · [[file:../30-reflections/30.20-practical-learning-human-centered-ai.org][Seeing the Difficulty First]]

The reading continues with the [[https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/顧亭林文集#天下郡國利病書序][preface to Benefits and Ills of the Regions]] and [[https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/顧亭林文集#郡縣論一][Essays on Prefectures and Counties, 1–3]], moving from authorship to local circumstances and arrangements for doing things. These working English titles refer to transcriptions listed in the supplements of volume 6 and in volume 1 of Gu Tinglin Wenji. The page does not identify their transcription's underlying edition; they must not inherit the Siku edition label of the Rizhilu material. This is a limited reading of these pieces, not a claim to have examined the entire regional compilation.

We also read [[https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/顧亭林文集#錢糧論上][Money and Grain, part one]], [[https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/顧亭林文集#錢糧論下][part two]], and county essays [[https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/顧亭林文集#郡縣論五][five]] and [[https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/顧亭林文集#郡縣論九][nine]], all transcribed in volume 1 on the same page. These working titles identify readings about what people obtain and need, additional burdens, personal concerns and different paths. We examine Gu's observations and arguments; their quantities, monetary claims and proposed policy effects have not been independently checked against other historical evidence here.

* Context

Approaching Gu through usefulness alone can make him sound as though every piece of writing needs an account of its return. Read together, these entries ask more: what world does writing address, whose hardship has its author noticed, where do its claims come from, and can they bear rereading? This is a morally and politically committed view of writing, not a neutral formula for efficiency. Nor can volume 19 stand for the whole of Gu's learning.

** Attending to hardship: what has not yet found its way into words

Among the purposes named in the opening entry are illuminating the Way, recording affairs of government, attending to people's hardship, and speaking well of their good deeds. The concern with hardship draws writing toward lives that may not appear in an imposing argument. Learning takes place not only between an author and books, but also in an encounter with people who write no books.

That encounter does not make representation reliable by itself. Gu's political and ethical setting is not a modern institution of participatory design. Asking whether AI hears difficulties absent from a form is our extension; requiring people to be able to correct a system's account of them is not a rule we can attribute to him. Above all, attending does not mean acquiring ever more private information. Caring about someone unheard differs from building a comprehensive profile of them.

** Quantity and the difficulty of authorship: care is neither length nor elapsed time

The entry on quantity refuses to let abundance establish substance. The entry on authorship places the problem within the time taken to write: even major works prepared with sustained effort may omit something; haste in pursuit of a book or a reputation can leave unreliable claims to later readers. Gu discusses revision and reluctance to publish. The difficulty is not merely getting words onto a page, but answering for them.

This offers a conversation with generative systems, not a verdict that fast writing must be ignorant. AI may help people find, compare and arrange material; it may also help someone unable to write fluently express experience otherwise unheard. What cannot be omitted is checking, discrimination and the possibility of challenge, not a performance of labor. A slowly written book, conversely, is not thereby free of error.

** Speaking frankly: help must allow the thought that something should not be done

The entry on frank speech first quotes Zhang Zai, then discusses allowing ordinary people to speak about shortcomings in government, education and custom. The quoted words must therefore not be presented as Gu's own aphorism. Nor is the entry a complete modern argument for rights of expression.

It nevertheless leaves a sharp question for us. If a system excels at making suggestions agreeable and disagreements acceptable, can it still accommodate “this should not happen”? In a modern service relationship, translating every protest into an experience to optimize may evade the very question of power and responsibility that prompted it. Rights of refusal and arrangements for appeal require our own arguments; citing an old book does not establish them.

** Writing beyond one's time: patience without immunity from later judgment

The final entry considers how words and proposals may matter to later generations, resisting judgment solely by immediate adoption. It allows us to take seriously research without an immediate use, painstaking work, and opinions long left unanswered.

Yet endurance or eventual adoption does not establish goodness. The historical proposals in this entry have distinct institutional settings and consequences; lasting influence must not automatically become a benefit to all. Applied to AI, future gains cannot excuse present harm. A long view needs patience, but also room for later people to judge differently.

** Leaving knowledge to later people: contribution need not be invention alone

The entry on the difficulty of authorship asks both whether a work reaches something predecessors missed and whether later readers need it. Gu makes distinct judgments about books compiled from other thinkers. His discussion cannot simply become approval of all contemporary compilation, teaching and reformulation. Using it to consider AI-assisted sharing of ordinary experience requires acknowledging our own judgment of a changed situation.

Today, an explanation may propose no new theory yet make scattered, difficult or narrowly held knowledge usable. That can still contribute. Its contribution needs an account of the difficulty it addresses, not the word “original” alone and not view counts alone. Fidelity to sources, omitted conditions and recognition of others' work remain part of the question.

Benefit and harm therefore need not end at restraint. Helping people who struggle to write articulate experience, helping beginners find an intelligible entrance, and enabling later readers to correct an earlier account are positive possibilities worth pursuing. Gu did not describe how generative AI might accomplish them. That direction is our contemporary claim, with its effects still to be examined.

** After attending to hardship, who may say what counts as benefit?

The opening entry names illuminating the Way and recording affairs of government as well as attending to hardship. Benefit is not a conclusion that automatically emerges after collecting individual preferences. It involves an author's judgment about what deserves transmission and opposition. Turning Gu into a designer awaiting user feedback would omit something substantial, and contestable, in the text.

For contemporary AI, we propose a further requirement: concern cannot remove the standing of those described to correct the account. Making a request compelling by portraying someone's life only through deprivation may conflict with their understanding of themselves. Yet this cannot mean that only those with direct experience may speak or that every personal account settles a shared question. Facts, shared consequences and absent people still belong in the discussion.

This calls for continuing argument, neither abolishing the author's judgment nor giving concern final interpretive authority. The [[file:../30-reflections/30.320-learning-in-ordinary-life.org][reflection on ordinary people practising with AI]] asks experience and general explanation to remain open to each other's questions, granting neither automatic priority to a model nor exemption from scrutiny to an experienced practitioner.

** The regional preface: keeping material, including where it no longer fits

The preface describes reading histories, local gazetteers, collected writings and official submissions, recording what Gu found. It also acknowledges that many accounts concern earlier times and that terrain and local customs may no longer correspond to the present. He leaves a draft for later readers to judge and select from. Its significance lies not only in collection, but in not treating collected material as work already completed on later people's behalf.

Our continuing question is whether the conditions on which an account depended still hold when it is used. This need not overturn general knowledge in every new place. It distinguishes a claim's earlier grounds from its present applicability: connected judgments, but not interchangeable ones. AI-assisted arrangement of older material should leave visible which dates, places and circumstances need checking. Removing their origins may make the prose smoother while making judgment harder.

This reading does not certify Gu's materials or turn his account of collecting them into a comprehensive description of actual places. In present-day practice, even proximity does not establish shared circumstances. One person can visit the same community room on a weekday afternoon; another has only a brief interval after a night shift. Considering suitability means hearing such differences, not generating an increasingly detailed schedule for everyone.

** The county essays: willingness needs arrangements that make action possible

The first essay criticizes pervasive control, layers of supervision and elaborate paperwork that leave local officials intent on avoiding fault rather than improving conditions. The second proposes long tenure, local appointments and hereditary office, among other arrangements. The third considers competence through fields, waterways, schools and related matters, ultimately asking whether people can pursue their livelihoods contentedly. Gu asks not only for dedication but how office and authority shape conduct.

We cannot accept all his answers along with that question. Hereditary office and family succession, severe punishments and a ruler's view of the governed stand apart from present commitments to equal participation and protection of rights. The third essay's comparison of caring for people with raising livestock is particularly revealing: concern for livelihood does not itself grant people equal standing to question their arrangements. Gu's reputation cannot dispose of these disagreements.

What we bring into the present is a question needing its own argument. When someone repeatedly struggles to accomplish something, we should ask not only whether they care, but whether they have time, resources and room to decide. AI that helps workers complete repetitive paperwork faster may offer immediate relief without answering why the same information is continually demanded. Conversely, fewer procedures are not inherently better. Some records allow someone harmed later to establish what happened; convenience for the person acting now cannot justify erasing them indiscriminately.

The proposal is therefore not for AI to decide institutional change. It can help people articulate the difficulty: what a step serves, whose time it consumes, who a change would affect and whether a less burdensome approach could preserve responsibility. Between organizing material and changing arrangements lie discussion among people and decisions made by those entitled to undertake them. A fluent recommendation cannot silently cross that distance.

** Money and Grain, part one: why having a harvest may not relieve hardship

Gu begins from accounts of hardship despite plentiful harvests, pointing to possession of grain without the silver demanded in taxation. He discusses different collection arrangements in relation to local trade, rather than assuming that more grain automatically relieves distress. His monetary history and policy judgments require further examination; the essay is not a prescription transferable to modern economic problems.

For our discussion, the suggestive question is how what someone produces becomes something they can use, exchange or retain. A craftsperson's ability does not itself bring an opportunity to explain it to someone who needs it. Even a clear explanation does not make that person able to pay or participate. Moving from grain and silver to ability and opportunity is our analogy, not a claim that their economic mechanisms are identical.

AI can help make experience intelligible and give a hesitant speaker material to discuss. But being noticed, finding willing participants and receiving compensation remain different events. When no response comes, better expression cannot be the only advice. The need may have been misunderstood, supporting conditions may be absent, or this route may not currently suit the person.

** Money and Grain, part two: what people pay is not all visible

The second essay examines additional levies under the name of silver-melting loss and describes different burdens on small households and influential taxpayers. Gu also explains officials' conduct through conditions of collection and transport. Rather than identifying greed alone, he asks what makes taking more easier and some people's objections harder. His explanations of regional differences and causation remain historical arguments to examine, not verified general laws established by vivid prose.

Our present question concerns work outside the stated fee: who organizes material, explains errors or goes back and forth for someone unfamiliar with the process? Such work is not all unjustified, but it cannot disappear from a claim of convenience. Equal prices need not mean comparable burdens. Fragmented time, limited support or difficulty objecting may make participation more costly.

This connection does not equate AI service charges with historical taxation. It asks who carries burdens beyond their formal names. AI might help a person explain inconsistent requirements and avoid repeated explanations. Yet requiring extensive records and professional vocabulary before responding could turn seeking help into another job.

** County essay five: personal concerns and shared affairs

The fifth essay acknowledges people's concern for their families and children, attempting to make officials' interests in themselves and their descendants a reason to care for a locality. Its account of the public remains directed toward imperial government and tied to inheritable office. It is neither modern public interest nor proof that private interests naturally produce common benefit.

We make a smaller claim of our own. Someone may care about others and hope that their work also supports a stable life. Neither motive must first erase the other. Charging for teaching or receiving payment for preparing material does not remove its contribution. Who pays whom, and whether someone unable to pay has a feasible entrance, still need discussion. Recognizing legitimate returns does not exempt their means or other people's circumstances from examination.

** County essay nine: more than one worthwhile path

Within a traditional order of scholars, the ninth essay distinguishes those unwilling to hold office who become teachers from those seeking public employment for their learning and abilities. It opposes unremitting competition for official distinction. These roles and their conditions of entry retain historical limits; the essay cannot become a present-day claim to equal occupational choice for everyone.

It nevertheless gives us a reason not to hurry every new ability toward the same destination. We ask whether AI can help people find fitting ways to participate. Someone wishing to expand an undertaking may seek support; someone wanting to do a bounded piece of work well need not count as having failed to grow. Small scale is not inherently noble, nor large scale inherently successful. People's desired lives and what those involved can jointly undertake matter.

* Interpretation Boundaries

“Practical learning” is a reading direction here, not a modern evaluative vocabulary jointly set out by these five entries. Tao3k's “benefit-harm inquiry” is likewise a modernization. Gu offers no AI scorecard. Who benefits, who maintains the service, who cannot leave, and who may disagree still require specific arguments and arrangements.

A view of public writing cannot cover all life without distinction. Private notes, idle reading, rest and words never meant for publication need not account for public benefit. Modern commitments to equal participation, privacy and exit also cannot simply be deduced from this text. We draw questions from it while retaining the possibility of disagreement.

Practical learning does not mean merely deploying, transacting or completing tasks. Naming missing conditions, acknowledging that a proposal cannot work, or giving good reasons to stop may be more practical than generating another plan. But claiming to exercise restraint cannot excuse abandoning work already accepted.

* Interpretation Chain

Five entries in volume 19, the regional preface, county essays 1–3, 5 and 9, and both parts of Money and Grain → this interpretation with its context and limits → modern claims in [[file:../30-reflections/30.20-practical-learning-human-centered-ai.org][Seeing the Difficulty First]] and [[file:../30-reflections/30.320-learning-in-ordinary-life.org][Bringing Learning into Life]]. A shared principle would still require a separately written Principle explaining counterexamples and conditions, followed by a Governance decision about admission. Historical evidence, philosophical argument, normative content and admission have different responsibilities. The chain cannot be reversed to claim that Gu proved the current architecture.

* Further Conversation

If writing should benefit the world, must every piece of writing prove its use? Reading the entry against valuing quantity readily brings today's easily generated long texts to mind. But turning the reminder into “do not write anything useless” could also erase unfinished notes, unsent letters, and a few lines intended for nobody else.

Such words may change nothing immediately. Yet someone hesitating over a sentence may discover what they cannot let go of. We should not invent a public benefit for those lines, as though tonight's writing needed a promise of future usefulness. This continues an argument with Gu's view of writing; it does not assign our account of private life to him.

Conversely, valuing private writing does not excuse floods of unchecked public output that consume other people's attention. Words kept in a drawer and the same words distributed by a system enter different relationships. Who spends time reading and who may be misled remain questions that freedom of expression alone does not settle.

An inquiry into benefit needs its own limits. Writing offered as public help can be asked what help it provides. Words someone keeps for themselves need not immediately produce a result. The [[file:../30-reflections/30.290-beginning-something-unfamiliar.org][question of why liking must lead to learning]] faces a related difficulty: how does a worthwhile demand avoid becoming an instruction for every part of life?

There is another question: if someone offers no new theory, only ways of doing things acquired over years, is their account worth keeping? Gu's demands on authorship were not written for every present-day teaching note. We cannot claim that proximity to life satisfies all his criteria. But some retelling and organizing deserve an argument of their own: they may let particular people understand, use and correct knowledge for the first time. The absence of a new term does not make that work negligible.

Private writing need not establish a public return, while publicly teaching material carries responsibility for accuracy and conditions of use. The two need not conflict. The [[file:../30-reflections/30.320-learning-in-ordinary-life.org][imagined encounter between Zhou and a beginner]] asks how experience can deserve careful transmission without making beneficial sharing a demand that everyone turn life into distributable content.

Reading the difficulty of authorship again, even works prepared through a lifetime can contain errors. We cannot casually equate later readers' need for a work with a prohibition on their changing it. Gu does not propose a contemporary institution of collaborative revision here. The following is our judgment: taking earlier labor seriously may include carefully identifying where it remains insufficient.

An account survives; later readers avoid some earlier labor by using it, then encounter conditions its author never faced. Addition and correction need not be betrayal. Conversely, erasing its origin as though all understanding began with the latest editor obscures how knowledge came about. Knowledge worth transmitting needs both a history and a future; an unalterable version need not be what holds them together.

AI's capacity to organize can help this continuation: bringing scattered questions back to relevant passages so that a discovery does not remain confined to a private conversation. But an account arranged as consensus has not thereby settled what is true. If a hesitant speaker's question disappears, or an old error returns in fluent wording, abundance of expression may still conceal a gap in understanding. This is our question to our own writing, not Gu's judgment of AI.

The [[file:../30-reflections/30.320-learning-in-ordinary-life.org][imagined notes reaching another community room]] also gives benefit to later people a concrete demand: who noticed an omission, explained a difference or undertook to send a correction to known users? Their work may never appear on a cover, yet participates in making knowledge usable. Valuing transmission while praising only the original author and overlooking later care has not fully attended to the people involved.

The community-room story admits another question. A worker helps a resident complete a form, and the request can finally proceed. Has the worker done well? Certainly that help should not be dismissed because it leaves the whole arrangement unchanged. But if the difficulty returns daily, we can also ask why people must repeatedly seek a favor to pass through this door. Respect for workers need not require preserving every old procedure. Concern for residents cannot be fulfilled by blaming a worker without authority to change it either.

Gu makes arrangements visible, yet his own arrangements need not be ones we would adopt. This is precisely where conversation matters. We can agree that difficulty should not all be assigned to individuals while opposing a family's lasting control of local affairs. We can value nearby experience while insisting that strangers to local relationships should also receive fair treatment. Reading an old book is not finding someone who always agrees with us. Particular disagreements can help us state more clearly the claims we ourselves will undertake.

If Zhou asks for payment after teaching once, has his earlier generosity changed its character? If he never asks but gradually has no time, will others think him insufficiently committed? Following the fifth essay's recognition of personal concerns, we want this awkwardness brought into conversation. We also part company with the historical proposal: payment should rest on particular work and shared agreements, not turn a public undertaking into someone's inheritable family possession.

Discussion may show that people can afford only an occasional session, or that someone should seek more suitable resources. This need not become a moving story of resolution. What matters is that Zhou need not conceal exhaustion to remain generous, and a learner need not feel ashamed of being unable to pay. AI can help calculate and explain, but how they treat each other still requires them to speak. The conversation now asks not only whether writing is useful, but whether useful people can avoid being used up.
