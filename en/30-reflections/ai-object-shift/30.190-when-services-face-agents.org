* Question
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: philosophy.reflection.30.190-when-services-face-agents-en
:END:
When airlines, restaurants, shops, calendars and public services receive a direct request not from a person operating
a screen but from an agent searching, negotiating and acting for someone, whom should a design standard treat as its
object? Is it enough for the service to be fast and reliable for the agent to call?
* Argument
When an agent becomes the direct object, the question is neither how to make an interface easier for machines alone
nor how to return to a traditional frame containing only the person at a screen. One action must be followed through
its caller, source of entrustment, affected parties, executing labor and public rules.
** Traditional Object and Applicability Boundary
Traditional GUI design usually assumes a person in view: the person sees choices, acts, receives feedback and can
return after error. Traditional API design assumes a relatively stable software caller whose developers understand
fields, errors and business relations in advance. Both contain valuable experience. Neither fully addresses a caller
that interprets natural language, composes tools for the occasion, represents different principals and negotiates with
another agent.

Inherited standards can still ask whether a control is reachable, information clear and an interface consistent. They
cannot alone answer who stands behind an agent request, how long authority lasts, whether affected parties appear, how
machine-made commitments return to people, or whether a service silently changes price and choice for a “machine
customer.”
** Object Shift
The direct interaction object may now be an agent. It reads capability descriptions, compares structured results,
calls tools, handles errors and continues. A service need not draw a human page for it first; the app made for people
may appear only during exception, review or dispute.

The direct object is not the whole object. Behind the agent is a principal. Beside that person are companions and
co-bearers. At the other end are people who answer calls, prepare food, change tickets and handle appeals. Around them
are market rules and public obligations. A design that optimizes only agent completion makes these people “external
conditions” even when they are where consequences land.

** Judgment
The present judgment is that a service may treat an agent as its direct interaction object, but not as its only design
subject. A new standard should constrain both machine-understandable capability boundaries and consequences people can
bear. It need not imitate GUI for an agent. It must make authority, cost, side effects, reversibility, partial completion
and completion evidence correctly usable by the agent and translatable back to relevant people.

The center is therefore neither “is the page easy to use?” nor “is the protocol easy to call?” It is how a chain of
action preserves meaning and responsibility: who states which purpose, under whose identity and authority the agent
acts, what the service changes, who is affected and how failure returns to someone able to address it.

** What an agent-facing standard needs

First, capability needs clear boundaries. A service states prerequisites, cost, changed state, reversal period and the
possibility of partial completion rather than offering only a success entrance.

Second, authority cannot be inferred from ability. Knowing how to buy a ticket does not authorize payment. Access to a
family calendar does not authorize rescheduling every member. A service needs to identify whom an agent represents,
the scope of this entrustment and rights another party must supply.

Third, results must return to people. A machine-readable receipt can be detailed. The human account need not repeat
every call, but it does state cost, commitment, effect, unfinished work and next step. If only an agent can interpret
the log, the person loses a place in dispute.

Fourth, an agent honestly identifies itself. When it calls for a reservation or negotiates, the other side should know
that it is interacting with an agent, whom the agent represents and how to reach an authorized person. Convenience
must not depend on impersonation.

Fifth, a service should neither add endless obstacles because a machine does not tire nor use the data behind an agent
for invisible differential price, ranking or persuasion. Human departure from the page does not end market power.

Sixth, a standard accommodates stopping and the undecided. Two agents finding a feasible result quickly does not mean
the relevant people made a commitment. Machine coordination needs draft, hold and awaiting-consent states.
** Argument and Debate Scenarios
The following scenes test the judgment that an agent may be the direct object without becoming the only subject. They
also show why the same technical shape needs different standards in different living relations.

| Shopping agent bargains with merchant agent | It can compare actual total cost continuously and enforce a budget | Platforms may infer ability to pay, personalize price and hide alternatives | Ranking and price difference are challengeable, interests visible and exit unpunished |
| Agent applies for public service | It may help with language, cognition or mobility barriers | A mistaken agent can widen exclusion and automated rejection can resist appeal | Reasons return to the applicant, human redress is reachable and agent use creates no unequal treatment |

An opposing view says that once a principal chooses an agent, the service need only verify credentials and execute
correctly. This may hold for low-risk private read-only queries. Once an action affects shared property, another
person's time, eligibility or public opportunity, “recognize only the caller” pushes social consequence outside the
protocol. Standards should converge by consequence rather than placing the same weight on every request.
** Principle and Governance Boundary
This remains a Reflection. It proposes a judgment and candidate contents for standards concerned with multiple objects;
it does not write them directly into a Charter Principle. A rule requiring agent identity disclosure or specifying
whose consent is required needs a separately authored Principle with scope, conflicting rights, exceptions and failure
modes.

Governance owns a different question: who may change a standard, which evidence admits or revises it, how conflicts of
interest are disclosed, and how appeal and incidents enter its lifecycle. Discussion of “standards” here must not mix
content judgment with governance authority.

* Counterexamples
First, stable machine-to-machine interaction has long worked without people understanding every technical step. A
standard that translates the whole process on every occasion defeats automation. Material effects and exceptions need
to appear, not protocol noise.

Second, requiring an agent to reveal its principal too broadly may violate privacy and endanger anonymous help,
sensitive inquiry or people under coercion. Identity should be proportionate to required authority; universal real-name
disclosure is not an answer.

Third, a service cannot know every relation its caller omitted. A standard must not pretend technical systems can
exhaust affected parties. It can require stopping, asking or limiting action around known effects and reasonably
discoverable relations.

Fourth, extra constraints on agents can protect incumbents and exclude small or open agents. Safety and responsibility
requirements that cannot be publicly verified or are too costly may concentrate the new entrance in a few companies.
* Provisional Conclusion
When a service faces an agent directly, the traditional “user” splits into several positions. The agent is a real
interaction object but not the final source of value and right. The principal supplies purpose but does not own every
affected person. A new standard must follow the action chain to see both how machines understand capabilities and how
consequences return to people, labor and institutions.

This is design after an object shift, not automation layered on an old app. It permits services without GUI while
requiring that understandable commitments, proportionate authority, challengeable differences, explicit failure and
reachable redress still exist after GUI.
* Questions That Remain
Questions remain about how several agents prove they have not duplicated a commitment, how stolen agent identity is
handled, how public services accept agent help without discriminating against people who lack it, and who keeps the
minimal sufficient record when model provider, service platform and payment party all participate.

A deeper question is whether people can still understand markets, public rules and possible paths when services
increasingly optimize for agents rather than people; whether service workers can refuse or limit machine calls; and
whether the next generation sees only the world selected by a personal agent. The topic must continue to change from
these places that “call success rate” cannot represent.
