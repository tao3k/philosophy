* Question
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: reflection-practical-learning-human-centered-ai-en
:END:

How can technology answer to real human problems and consequences without reducing human value to an optimizable metric?

[[file:../40-sources/40.20-gu-yanwu-practical-learning.org][source note]] · [[file:../10-charter/10.70-practical-learning.org][target thematic Charter]]

* Argument

Statecraft requires knowledge to enter real problems; a benefit-harm test requires it to answer to consequence. Performance improvement says what a system can do better, not what change that capability creates in whose life. Technical value must name beneficiaries, burden bearers, altered dependencies, exit possibilities, real effects, and time horizons.

value claim = benefit + burden + agency + dependency + reversibility + time
#+end_example

Human-centered cannot mean only friendly UI or a final human click. Human agency includes understanding what is happening, refusing an unreasonable objective, withdrawing authority, choosing alternatives, and retaining the capacity to form one's own judgment. A system that increases efficiency while creating inescapable dependency may be useful in the short term and diminish practical capacity over time.

Every high-impact optimization therefore needs a benefit-harm account: the concrete problem claimed to improve, evidence and observation window, distribution of benefits and costs, populations not measured, reversibility of failure, ownership of maintenance and migration costs, and the options that remain if the technology stops. This is not an ethical score. It prevents one metric from monopolizing the meaning of value.

* Counterexamples

Infrastructure improvement may lack a direct user narrative, yet still alter maintenance burden, failure radius, supply-chain dependence, and future options. A benefit-harm test can admit indirect value without inventing immediate user benefit.

Research may have no near-term deployment effect and still have epistemic value. It should state its exploratory stage, opportunity cost, and unestablished practical value rather than presenting future possibility as present impact.

Stakeholder interests can conflict and may not share one unit of comparison. A benefit-harm test does not automatically solve value conflict. It keeps conflict, power difference, and uncompensable loss visible and returns final choice to a process with legitimate authority.

* Provisional Conclusion

Technical value must pass a real-world benefit-harm test: not only whether performance improved, but who benefits, who is harmed, what dependencies form, how human options change, and whether long-term consequence can be observed and corrected. Metrics provide evidence but do not own the definition of value.

Practical learning here does not mean “deploy whatever works.” It requires thought and technology to answer to lived reality. Human-centered is not a product label; affected people retain standing to understand, challenge, refuse, exit, and participate in defining success.

* Promotion Criteria

Before promotion, test an efficiency gain that reduces agency, a short-term cost that improves resilience, and a conflict of benefits across groups. Evaluation must expose benefit, harm, unknowns, exit costs, and challenge paths, and show that distinct dimensions are not collapsed into one score.
