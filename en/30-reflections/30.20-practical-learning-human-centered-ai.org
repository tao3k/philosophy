* Question
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: reflection-practical-learning-human-centered-ai-en
:END:

Imagine a discussion about improving a shared courtyard. AI quickly produces a comprehensive proposal: weekend markets, evening films, classes for older residents, children's activities, registration, reminders and feedback. Someone is pleased to see scattered wishes brought together. Another person reaches the last page and asks, “It gets so hot here in the afternoon. Could we first have somewhere shaded to sit and talk?”

The question does not prove the proposal wrong. Some residents genuinely want activities; others worry about noise. What would the person who missed the meeting, and passes through each day in a wheelchair, want to say? This is an imagined scene, not an interview or an observed outcome. It asks how AI, already able to give an apparently complete account, might still discover what it has not asked.

* Argument

The difficult part of rereading Gu may not be agreeing that writing should be useful. It may be admitting that we do not yet know what use would mean for these particular people. Attending to hardship cannot remain an author's gesture of concern: it should bring a seemingly complete proposal back into contact with lives that resist its account. The following is a modern reflection prompted by an old text, not an attribution of participatory design, privacy or exit rights to Gu.

** Difficulties do not always arrive in the form of a question list

The person asking for shade may have painful knees and need somewhere to rest. Or perhaps they simply want to sit with a neighbor. They do not owe a medical history before they may ask for a seat. Attending to someone's circumstances does not require them to explain themselves until a system can comfortably classify them.

AI can ask a clarifying question or offer possibilities that help someone recognize, “That is what I was trying to say.” This is real help. But getting a feeling right once does not confer lasting authority to describe the person. The account must remain revisable. “That is not what I mean” cannot be treated as resistance awaiting persuasion. Nor may a model turn absent residents into imagined supporters on the basis of age, occupation or purchase history.

Some things left unsaid need a more patient question, some need a visit to the place, and some are things people do not wish to disclose. These are not all missing information. If a request for shade becomes a permanent vulnerability profile, our attempt to get closer to a person may have added another burden.

** Beyond a proposal, there is ground that the sun actually reaches

AI can organize options for shade, help locate material information, and explain differences in cost and upkeep. It may help someone unaccustomed to writing applications turn a plain request into words others will read seriously. There is no need to belittle generation: expression no longer belonging only to those who write polished proposals could itself change the discussion.

A beautiful rendering, however, does not establish when afternoon sun reaches this spot, whether a passage will obstruct a wheelchair, who can authorize construction, or whom to call when something breaks. What needs observing must be observed; permission and budget cannot be supplied by sentences that sound like promises. Not everyone must check every detail personally. Delegation can lighten the work, provided the person accepting it distinguishes what is established from what is only imagined.

A wish, a possible approach, and the conditions for carrying it out are different things. Their distance from one another is often precisely where help matters. [[file:../40-sources/40.95-sen-capability-and-opportunity.org][Substantive opportunity]] directs attention to missing time, money, company or avenues of participation, not merely insufficient effort. Finding reliable support can be worth more than ten further ideal plans. Where support cannot yet be found, the absence should remain visible rather than become the person's failure.

** Questioning quantity must not silence people who struggle to write

Gu considers both quantity and the difficulty of authorship. Today, that can sound like an instruction to shorten AI answers. Yet a brief, dogmatic conclusion can be irresponsible, while a longer explanation may finally let someone enter a discussion they could not follow.

The distinction is whether more words bring more understanding. Are ten proposals one proposal with different names? Does an account of a resident come from that person or from a familiar story in the model? After three revisions, is the original doubt still visible? Checking and revision need not make work look laborious. They give error a chance to be found. Speed is neither a fault in itself nor an exemption from scrutiny.

A comparison table can save reading time; a careful record can explain to later residents why the cheapest approach was rejected. Conversely, dozens of unverifiable pages after every meeting may save only the writer's time while transferring reading and judgment to others. Usefulness must consider those people too, without inventing a single score capable of settling every gain and loss.

** Frank speech is not disagreement made more agreeable

Suppose someone says, “I do not want activities here every evening.” A model may help clarify whether the concern is noise, timing or passage, but it cannot assume the person merely struggles to express themselves and will eventually accept the proposal. The objection may concern the proposal itself, not the communication.

Service systems readily absorb disagreement into “improving the experience”: change the time, invite again, offer a gentler explanation. Sometimes this helps. Sometimes it prevents a refusal from ever being recognized. Gu's entry on frank speech prompts a pause: if participation can improve execution but cannot question the direction, has half of it already disappeared?

An objection does not automatically confer a veto over everyone else. Decisions should nevertheless come through an authorized, challengeable shared process, not a model's declaration that one preference is more reasonable. People need to know who decides, who bears costs and how to object; those unable to attend need a genuinely usable route to speak. A feedback button that can never affect a decision is not enough.

** A longer view does not cancel what is happening now

A shaded place might receive few visits. That alone does not make it pointless: perhaps it finally lets a few people who cannot walk far come downstairs. But low use cannot simply be declared a triumph of leaving room either. The place might be poorly situated or still inaccessible. Observation and people's own accounts remain necessary.

Gu's writing beyond one's time cautions against judging everything by adoption this month. Understanding, trust and shared life may take longer to change. Yet “it will be good in the long run” is also easy to say. Who continues the upkeep, who bears the noise and who carries the cost of trying cannot all be deferred to a distant future.

Performance, cost and use can therefore provide evidence without deciding goodness alone. A faster AI deserves examination not only for time saved but also for harder-to-detect errors and dependence on a system people cannot leave. We may also need to ask what remains for the courtyard and its residents if the service stops.

** These questions must not turn life into an assignment

Someone sits under a tree without meeting a neighbor, recovering productive capacity or having an idea worth recording. They may still have spent a good afternoon. A public facility has costs and responsibilities to explain; the person using it does not therefore owe an account of personal gain.

The demands on AI and on a person receiving help differ. A system offering help should explain what it has and has not done. The person need not continually prove improvement. One person wants to learn a craft; another wants one fewer obligation today. Care, delegation and help with ordinary tasks can provide real support. Converting every ease into another goal produces a different kind of busyness. [[file:30.140-leaving-room-for-life.org][Leaving Room for Life]] continues the question: once a matter is settled, can we refrain from prescribing what life must do next?

* Counterexamples

This essay cannot require every research project to begin with a moving user story. Infrastructure may matter through easier maintenance, smaller consequences of failure or freedom from dependence on one supplier. Such indirect changes can have evidence without an invented beneficiary.

Research with no near-term application also has epistemic worth. It should explain what it explores, the resources involved and what remains unknown about its use, rather than buy patience with a promise of a necessarily better life. Allowing uncertainty differs from refusing scrutiny.

Interests in shared life may not be reconcilable. Some people need quiet; others lack company. More care may require everyone to contribute. This essay promises no proposal that satisfies all and gives no model authority to translate uncompensable loss into an apparently optimal number. Some decisions must be made amid explicit responsibilities and disputes, remaining open to correction.

* Provisional Conclusion

We want AI to become more capable so that some previously difficult things can be done and some previously difficult experiences can be expressed. Capability does not answer what the present difficulty is, who has not been heard or who pays for the accomplishment.

This is the practical learning we pursue with Gu: let ideas enter the world of things, and let things contradict ideas. Responsibility to people requires more than a final confirmation click. Understanding, challenge, refusal, a change of approach and departure must become possible in practice. Nor must people submit their whole lives to an evaluative method.

If a shaded place really is built, should we arrange something for it next? Now that space exists, perhaps people could read together, talk or help one another. These are not bad wishes. But the person who first wanted somewhere to sit may wish to organize none of them.

Gu leaves us dissatisfied with concern that never leaves the page. Reading [[file:../40-sources/40.320-laozi-action-and-room.org][Laozi chapter 11 and Wang Bi's commentary]] suggests another thought: a room need not have everything arranged within it to be useful. The two do not combine into a procedure of building infrastructure and then stopping service. Seats need repair, passages need care, and someone may later ask whether something could be done together here.

The difficulty is that continuing care and continuing occupation can look alike. AI asking every day about activities may understand itself as caring while making a person explain each refusal. Offering nothing further may leave those with the least access outside. An invitation that is easy to decline and does not bring follow-up questioning may be worth trying. But the inviter cannot decide alone whether declining felt easy.

We cannot choose a position between active help and leaving room that is right forever. We can retain something concrete: promised care needs someone to carry it, while someone who never agreed to surrender their life does not owe cooperation. A courtyard can be maintained and still have an afternoon without an event. That is not a failure of shared life, but neither is it a success we have already demonstrated.

* Questions That Remain

The courtyard is a fictional entrance into clearer questions, not practical evidence. Further work should hear actual inconveniences and disagreements within people's consent, recording what they wish to share rather than asking for entire lives in the name of complete research. Objectors, people who leave and those unable to participate disappear especially easily from final accounts.

We still need to compare efficient help that is difficult to refuse, a change that costs more work now but is easier to maintain later, and decisions that cannot satisfy different groups at once. No single essay completes this work. The content of a principle, the way shared decisions are made and the facts supporting them require distinct scrutiny.
