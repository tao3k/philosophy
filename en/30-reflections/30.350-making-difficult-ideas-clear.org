* Question
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: philosophy.reflection.30.350-making-difficult-ideas-clear-en
:END:
When we discussed “something that aids reading is more valuable,” the comparison was not between paper and screens. It was between producing another article, analysis or piece of code and helping someone understand, judge and use material already before them. Here the phrase begins an inquiry; it is neither attributed to a famous author nor treated as a ranking valid in every circumstance.

The difficulty already appears in working life. The 2025 Stack Overflow survey records almost-correct solutions and time spent debugging AI-generated code among respondents' frustrations. It does not establish that more explanation would solve them, but it directs attention beyond time saved during generation. People still need to know what a result does, where reliance is warranted and where to begin investigating a problem. ([[https://survey.stackoverflow.co/2025/ai][Survey]]; [[file:../40-sources/40.330-ai-public-problems.org][Evidence and sample limits]])

Writing that feels human therefore deserves another look. Removing mechanical phrases and adding conversational language is not enough. Warm, natural prose can still leave someone lost; concise, precise prose need not be uncaring. Our concern is whether an explanation attends to its reader, rather than merely imitating a person speaking.
* Argument

This essay moves from reading assistance to the responsibilities of explanation: making difficult ideas approachable without hiding the conditions that matter. We read a specific explanation and preface by Feynman for possibilities and doubts, not as a method validated by fame. The [[file:../40-sources/40.350-feynman-explanation-and-teaching.org][source note]] identifies the reading and its limits.

** The writer's clarity still needs to meet a reader

The story of Bai Juyi testing his poems with an older woman takes writing beyond the author's desk. We treat it as a later anecdote, not a verified record of every act of composition. His own Preface to New Music Bureau Poems provides firmer ground: he asks for direct language that readers can understand, alongside matters checked and grounded enough to be credibly transmitted. Accessibility and factual grounds are not presented as mutually exclusive. ([[file:../40-sources/40.351-bai-juyi-clarity-and-credibility.org][Texts, anecdote and interpretive scope]])

Our extension is not to find one approving reader and declare an explanation clear for everyone. When someone does not understand, the explainer should also reconsider: was their question skipped, background assumed, or an unfamiliar meaning carried by a familiar word? Failed understanding cannot always be assigned to the reader.

Age and lack of professional training do not imply lack of insight. Someone unfamiliar with a report's terminology may know its subject's circumstances intimately. Their difficulty may indicate not only missing knowledge but something unclear or wrong in the account. Readers are not merely a test of style; they may bring facts the writer has yet to recognise.

** Accessibility means supplying a missing step, not thinning the subject

Some explanations are difficult because their words are unfamiliar. Others contain no unfamiliar words yet give us no way in. They introduce a concept without showing the problem that makes it worth introducing. A step available to the writer is silently assumed available to the reader.

Feynman's account of energy conservation uses counting blocks to introduce an invariant, then warns against treating energy as those blocks. The turn matters: an analogy offers access without being allowed to stand in for the thing explained. ([[https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_04.html][Chapter 4, section 1]])

Our writing lesson is not to invent a small story for everything. Code, statistical findings and research papers involve different relationships. A comparison table may serve better than a story; sometimes an exact definition must remain. Is the reader struggling with a word, the context, a jump in the reasoning, or the relevance to their question? One style of simplification cannot address all these difficulties.

A good explanation may be brief or need considerable preparation. Its measure is not simply words saved, but which connections the reader no longer has to supply alone.

** Redundancy says more without making more apparent

Restating a conclusion three ways and repeating it in a summary can increase length without increasing understanding. A reader may even search those near-identical sentences for a distinction that is not there.

Yet repetition can be considerate. An abstract account, a suitable example and a limit on its use each do different work. Reviewing a point may help someone listening, learning for the first time or returning after an interval. Reducing everything to keywords may merely transfer the explanatory work to them.

We propose asking whether a passage adds necessary grounds, distinctions or connections. What would the reader lose if it disappeared? If nothing, completeness need not keep it there. If what disappears is a condition determining whether the conclusion applies, concision cannot justify the cut.

** Who does the work of understanding?

Understanding involves work that is easy to overlook. When an explainer omits a term's context or buries a condition, readers must search, supply and guess. An insider might lose a moment; a newcomer might be unable to continue. Unnecessary complexity concerns not only style but who can use the knowledge.

Reducing that work does not mean settling every judgment on the reader's behalf. AI can organise materials, explain differences and bring important grounds into view. Which costs are acceptable and which purposes worth pursuing still belong in discussion among those concerned. We want less unnecessary obscurity, not less room for disagreement.

** Articles, data and code call for different kinds of reading

“Please explain” may still be too broad a request about a generated result. A longer description may never reach the actual difficulty. These are proposed directions for reading, not a compulsory procedure for every task.

| Material | What fluent presentation can conceal | What useful explanation can make visible |
|----------+--------------------------------------+-------------------------------------------|
| An analytical article | An unstated premise between evidence and conclusion | Which grounds support which claim, and what remains conjecture |
| A data report | Similar numbers produced by different counting rules | Who is counted or omitted, and what another denominator changes |
| Generated code | An example runs while crucial conditions remain untested | Required input conditions and which test checks which requirement |

Such explanations must return to the actual material. A plausible account of a design does not establish its author's intention; a lucid explanation of a report does not show that the underlying data was inspected. Reading assistance should bring checkable grounds closer, not wrap one fluent output in another.

Here AI has a positive possibility. It can cross terminological and language barriers, bring scattered definitions alongside a passage, compare materials in relation to a question, or make a complicated change history easier to follow. Discussions previously open mainly to insiders may become more accessible. Whether that happens requires readers' experience, not the explainer's declaration.

** Following an explanation is not yet the same as being able to judge

The moment something makes sense is valuable. It may begin learning. It does not tell us whether someone can recognise the relationship when conditions change.

In his preface, Feynman expresses reservations, even pessimism, about the course's results and identifies the difficulty of missing student feedback. Admiring his explanations and taking learning outcomes seriously can therefore coexist. ([[https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_91.html][Feynman's preface]])

Recent research makes the distinction concrete. One 2025 preprint reports better comprehension-question performance with a particular simplification method. Another finds better comprehension from human-written plain-language summaries despite generally similar subjective ratings. The first compares original and simplified texts; the second compares different sources of plain-language summaries. They are not opposing verdicts on the same comparison. ([[https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.01980][Simplification experiment]]; [[https://arxiv.org/html/2505.10409v1][Summary study]])

Clarity cannot therefore be judged only by who wrote a text or how many people liked it. What changed, for whom, and towards what kind of understanding? Samples, methods and limits are gathered in the [[file:../40-sources/40.352-reading-understanding-evidence.org][research note]], rather than repeated beneath every paragraph.

We need not turn every reading into an examination. Someone trying to learn a judgment might be invited to compare two accounts, locate a remaining difficulty or consider a changed condition. When they struggle, the response should help locate the difficulty rather than insist that it has already been explained. Someone seeking reliable completion, however, has not thereby agreed to take a course.

Further investigation should distinguish experience from performance: willingness to read, grasp of important relationships, recognition of errors and time required. These may support one another or diverge. They should not disappear into one score for being more human. Such comparisons are proposals to investigate, not learning outcomes established by this essay.

** Clear public information should do more than make compliance easier

A Dutch government-letter study found that expert-assessed ChatGPT simplifications improved answers to comprehension and action-related questions. This does not establish better real-world outcomes, but places explanation beyond style: can people understand how a document concerns them? ([[https://aclanthology.org/2024.determit-1.15/][Government communication study]])

We should also ask questions the study does not answer for us. Does a notice make choices visible, explain when conditions do not apply, and show where questions or objections can be raised? Clarifying instructions while removing exceptions or routes of appeal may make compliance easier without improving people's understanding of their situation.

Likewise, research limitations and untested conditions in a code change should not disappear for the sake of flow. Clarity can support cooperation while permitting refusal, pause and reconsideration. Helping people act need not mean securing the publisher's desired response.

Checking should also become practicable. A CSCW 2023 study using a maze and simulated AI finds that factors including explanation difficulty affect overreliance. Our extension is to help readers locate checkable relationships rather than add another generic account of why an answer is correct. This transfer still needs investigation in actual reading. ([[https://hci.stanford.edu/publications/2023/xai-cscw-2023.pdf][Research article]])

** Respect includes letting readers depart from our explanation

Explanation involves selection, but selection need not provide the only entrance. Readers should be able to return to a source, identify material that matters more to them or reject an analogy that does not fit their experience.

An inarticulate question still deserves care. Naming and classifying it too quickly may miss the difficulty behind it. AI can offer what it thinks the question is and invite correction without building a permanent cognitive profile of the person.

This gives leaving room a practical meaning: explain the core and keep further grounds accessible, without opening every branch in advance. Readers can continue or stop here. Every explanation need not end by assigning something else to learn.

Warmth also need not depend on pretence. Generated prose need not invent lived experience or feelings to earn trust. Hearing a question accurately, admitting uncertainty and patiently finding another grounded account are already ways of taking someone seriously.
* Counterexamples

Reading assistance is not inherently more valuable than generation. A usable result may give someone unable to perform a particular act of expression or implementation their first opportunity to participate. They need not first demonstrate complete understanding. Established delegation may also suit the moment better than a lengthy explanation.

Not all difficulty comes from poor expression. Some concepts require preparation; some derivations cannot be mastered through one analogy. Hiding this may leave readers blaming themselves later. Explaining what preparation remains is part of helping.

Technical terms and precise formulae can be easier than everyday language. An expert may need one exact difference, not an introduction. Conversely, shortening a text should not remove context needed by newcomers or people using assistive reading methods.

If later observation finds that a rewrite increases confidence while making errors harder to detect or removing checkable grounds, we should reject that rewrite. A presentation that helps some readers and burdens others cannot be called universally accessible.
* Provisional Conclusion

We want writing that feels human to mean more than a style preference: does it attend to understanding, time and choice? Making difficult ideas clear does not make every subject simple. It helps people know what they are approaching, why they might believe it and how they can continue questioning.

This remains a Reflection. It changes no Principle or Governance requirement and creates no score based on word count or conversational tone. The next inquiry should compare explanations through actual materials and readers' difficulties, rather than asking AI alone to declare its own output understandable.

[[file:../40-sources/40.20-gu-yanwu-practical-learning.org][Gu Yanwu's discussions of writing and learning]] direct our attention to knowledge entering shared life. [[file:../40-sources/40.350-feynman-explanation-and-teaching.org][Feynman's explanation and self-questioning]] invite attention to the work of reaching a reader. They do not constitute one doctrine, and we do not invent agreement across time. The connection is our question: even well-grounded knowledge has not realised its usefulness merely by existing if those who need it cannot approach it.

Approachability does not require every idea to be immediately intelligible to everyone. Readers may need preparation; writers may explore subjects for which no mature explanation yet exists. Our objection is not to difficulty, but to using difficulty to protect authority unwilling to explain; not to accessible language, but to using it to erase what deserves attention.

Another tension remains: choosing what to explain first influences how a reader sees. We cannot pretend otherwise, yet abandoning guidance does not solve the problem. A more honest relationship might make its selections discussable, offer another route and allow a reader to reach a different judgment. Helping someone understand is not the same as securing agreement.

Bai Juyi's preface has an explicit admonitory purpose, not merely a wish for effortless prose. Borrowing his concern for intelligibility should not silently become making a publisher's intentions easier to accept. The [[file:../40-sources/40.351-bai-juyi-clarity-and-credibility.org][Bai Juyi note]] retains this distance: reaching someone through words and enabling them to question those words are connected but different demands. The latter requires our own argument, not an ancient author's endorsement.
* Questions That Remain

The comparison worth making concerns not only which version is preferred, but whether readers can better distinguish grounds, conditions and disagreements. Inquiry must respect the purpose of the occasion: learning, reference and delegation call for different help. Performance without tools cannot be the universal measure.
